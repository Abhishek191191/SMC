# 01: Supply & Demand: Institutional Zones

## The Problem
Many traders draw support and resistance lines based on where price has bounced multiple times. But every time price bounces, orders are consumed. A level touched five times is not strong; it is completely depleted. Retail buys the 6th touch, and it breaks instantly.

## The Story: The Wholesale Warehouse
Imagine a wholesale warehouse selling iPhones at $500. Buyers flood in. The warehouse sells out of its 10,000 units in minutes. The price moves up to $800 elsewhere. Weeks later, the warehouse gets a new shipment of 5,000 units at $500. When price returns to $500, those units will vanish instantly. This is a Demand Zone. It is not a line on a chart; it is a physical location of unfilled wholesale orders.

## The Concept
Supply and Demand (S&D) zones are areas where a massive imbalance between buyers and sellers originated. Unlike Order Blocks (which are specific candles), S&D zones often encompass a broader structural base (Rally-Base-Drop, Drop-Base-Rally).

### Why It Works
It works based on the concept of residual liquidity. When institutions accumulate massive positions during a "Base" phase, they cannot get fully filled before price explodes away (Rally or Drop). The unexecuted limit orders remain in the book at that base zone.

## How It Works
1. **The Base:** Price consolidates tightly. This is the institution building their position.
2. **The Imbalance:** Price aggressively leaves the base, creating FVGs.
3. **The Return:** Price eventually drops back to the base. The resting limit orders absorb the sell pressure, creating a high-probability bounce.

## Key Takeaways
- **Freshness is everything.** The first time price returns to an S&D zone is the highest probability. By the third touch, the orders are consumed.
- **Strength of departure.** A zone that caused a 500-point rally is vastly more important than one that caused a 50-point rally.
